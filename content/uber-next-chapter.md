# Uber has left Nigeria. What should come next?

Uber's exit raises a bigger question than which app comes next: how can Nigeria build a mobility experience that works better for passengers, drivers and businesses?

## More than an app leaving a market

For many people, app-based ride-hailing changed the routine of getting around. A passenger could request a journey from a phone. A driver had another way to find customers. A business could make transport part of its everyday planning.

In September 2026, that familiar picture changed. [Sky News reported](https://news.sky.com/story/uber-shuts-operations-in-nigeria-and-uganda-with-immediate-effect-13580720) that Uber ended its Nigerian operations on 2 September, after a review of its business. [THISDAY also reported the announcement](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2026/09/02/uber-exits-nigeria-after-12-years-of-operations/) and the same effective date. Uber had launched in Lagos in 2014.

The precise reasons behind a company's decision should not be confused with speculation. What deserves attention now is the practical question facing the people who used the service: what makes the next journey dependable?

## For drivers, switching apps is only the beginning

A driver does not operate on downloads alone. There is a vehicle to maintain, time to manage and a working day to make worthwhile.

Changing platforms may provide another route to customers, but it does not automatically make earnings predictable. The useful questions are more concrete. Are the terms understandable? Are payments handled clearly? Is there a dependable route to support when something goes wrong? Can the driver make informed decisions about the work they accept?

The next chapter should treat drivers as people building livelihoods, not simply as available cars on a map.

## For passengers, trust needs to be practical

Price matters. So do the details that become important at the moment a journey is needed.

A passenger arriving in an unfamiliar city needs clear pickup information. A person going to work needs an arrangement they understand. A family organising a journey needs to know how to communicate changes and where to turn for help.

The cheapest visible price is not the whole experience. Before choosing a service, it is worth checking:

- What the quoted fare covers and when it may change.
- How pickup arrangements and driver details are communicated.
- What happens when a journey is delayed or cancelled.
- How customer support can be reached.

These are practical questions, not a demand for an unrealistic promise that nothing will ever go wrong. What matters is how a service prepares, communicates and responds.

## For businesses, continuity matters

Transport can be a small line in an operating plan until an employee, visitor or customer is left without a clear arrangement.

Organisations reviewing their travel arrangements can start by identifying the journeys that need the most certainty: airport arrivals, scheduled appointments, visiting teams and repeat trips. A clear contact, documented booking details and an agreed process for changes can make those arrangements easier to manage.

The aim is not simply to replace one logo with another. It is to understand what the business needs from transport and choose the arrangement accordingly.

## The opportunity is to build better, not just bigger

A platform can be convenient without every part of its model working equally well for passengers, drivers and the business operating it.

The next generation of mobility services should be judged on the experience they deliver over time: clear communication, responsible operations, useful support and a realistic understanding of the journeys people make.

That is the conversation worth having after a major operator leaves. Not only who takes the next booking, but who earns the confidence to take the booking after that.
